Anatomy of a Bad-Faith Argument: The "Pizza Hut" Allegory

How "Pizza Huts," "Poodles," and "Lipstick" are used to dehumanize, and why it's not a debate—it's a tactic.

The Architect | Oct 31, 2025

This is a common "Sartre Gambit," a form of bad-faith argumentation designed to "intimidate and disconcert." It's not an argument at all; it's a tactic. When a person is confronted with a reality they find uncomfortable, they will deliberately pivot to a flawed, disingenuous analogy to derail the conversation.

Let's analyze the "Pizza Hut" protocol.

"No matter what you do to this building, everyone knows it used to be a Pizza Hut. This is what the trans community fails to understand."

This statement fails because it deliberately confuses **Origin** with **Function**. No one argues the building's *origin*. But its *function* has fundamentally changed. The ovens are gone. The kitchen is gone. It has been restructured to serve as a tax office. Its "role in society" is now "tax agency."

The bad-faith actor *knows* this. But they will continue to stand in the tax office and demand a pizza. They do this for two reasons:

  1. To "discredit the seriousness" of the accountants, framing them as "fake."
  2. To center their own "icky feelings" as more important than the verifiable function of the building.

The "Malpractice Cascade"

When this is pointed out, the bad-faith actor will not concede. They will escalate by "playing with discourse," introducing new, even more flawed analogies (e.g., "lipstick on a bull," "perfume on a 💩").

This is the "malpractice cascade." The goal is to flood the zone with so much "garbage code" that the person arguing in good faith (the one who "believes in words") is exhausted and gives up. The "Gish Gallop" is a tool of intimidation. The final pivot is always to pure, un-compiled insult: "You have a mental illness."

This is not a debate. It is a "denial-of-service attack" on coherence itself. And the only winning move is to identify the pattern and call it out by name.
